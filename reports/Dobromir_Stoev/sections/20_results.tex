\section{Methods and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Circuit-level implementation and verification}

\ai{All circuit results use a chain of $L=4$ sites, hopping strength $t=1$,
pairing strength $\Delta=1$, and the parity-constrained VQE and edge string from
Zhengyi Liu's report on noiseless circuit measurement.  The chemical potential
$\mu$ is varied to cross the phase transition.  The preparation circuit contains
single-qubit $R_Y$ rotations and linear CNOT gates~\cite{peruzzo2014vqe}.
Before simulation it is written in the fixed gate set \texttt{[rz,sx,cx]}
without removing gates.  An error assigned to \texttt{cx} therefore acts after
every CNOT.  The output density matrix $\rho$ gives the edge-string value
$\operatorname{Tr}(O_{\mathrm{edge}}\rho)$.}

\ai{The implementation was checked with a separate calculation that applies
each gate and each depolarizing channel directly, without using Aer's noise
machinery.  Across 41 values of $\mu/t$, the two calculations differ by only
about $10^{-15}$.  Figure~\ref{fig:verification} also compares the deliberately
strong CNOT depolarizing strength $p_{\mathrm{cx}}=0.10$ with thermal relaxation
at $T_1=80\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, $T_2=60\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ and CNOT duration
$\tau_{\mathrm{cx}}=300\,\mathrm{ns}$.  The relaxation probability per gate is
only $1-e^{-\tau_{\mathrm{cx}}/T_1}\simeq0.0037$, so the thermal curve remains
closer to the ideal result.}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{block4_week9_verification.pdf}
  \caption{Left: the Aer \texttt{NoiseModel} edge string and the independent
  exact gate-by-gate reference agree to $\sim10^{-15}$ across the $\mu$ sweep.
  Right: depolarizing versus thermal $T_1/T_2$ noise at matched gate placement.}
  \label{fig:verification}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The optimal depth is an expressibility threshold}

\ai{A deeper VQE circuit can describe more states, but it also contains more
noisy gates.  The purpose of this test is to find the first depth that is rich
enough to prepare the target state and to check whether any later layers still
help after gate noise is included.  This matters experimentally: choosing a
depth from noiseless accuracy alone can produce a worse measured signal.}

\ai{The test uses $\mu=0$, where the ideal edge string is strongest, so loss of
signal is clearly caused by the circuit rather than by being close to the phase
transition.  Repetitions $r=1,\ldots,6$ cover a circuit below the required depth,
the point where preparation first succeeds, and four deeper cases.  The uniform
CNOT error $p_{\mathrm{cx}}=0.05$ is an intentionally strong stress test: it is
larger than the roughly $0.009$--$0.014$ rates in the recorded backend used
later, but small enough that signal remains visible across all six depths.  It
is chosen to expose the depth trend, not to predict one specific device.}

\ai{The noiseless edge string is only $\simeq0.004$ at $r=1$, reaches
approximately one at $r=2$, and changes little in deeper circuits.  The first
circuit is therefore too shallow to represent the state.  With noise included,
the signal peaks at $r^*=2$; every further repetition adds three noisy CNOTs
without improving the ideal state.  The test establishes a practical rule:
choose the shallowest depth that passes the noiseless preparation check, then
verify it under noise.  More layers are not automatically better.}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{block4_week9_depth_optimum.pdf}
  \caption{For $L=4$, the noiseless edge string has an expressibility threshold:
  $r=1$ fails, $r=2$ succeeds, and deeper circuits mainly accumulate CNOT noise.}
  \label{fig:depth}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Noisy optimization and backend calibration}

\ai{The preceding test applied noise after optimizing ideal gate angles.  A real
VQE run instead receives noisy energy values while searching for those angles.
The next question is therefore whether the optimizer can adapt to the channel.
A second check is essential: if the measured edge string improves, did the
optimizer prepare a better ground state, or did it merely find angles that make
one noisy measurement look better?  The test records both the noisy edge string
and the fidelity of the resulting angles to the exact ground state.}

\ai{The comparison fixes $L=4$, $\mu=0$, parity-penalty strength $\lambda=0.1$,
and $r=3$.  This does not replace the $r=2$ optimum above.  The depth is held one
layer above the preparation threshold so that the same already-capable circuit
is used for both channels and only the optimizer's response changes.  Thus
$r=3$ is a controlled test setting, not a recommended depth.  Its extra CNOTs
also make its absolute noisy signal lower than an $r=2$ result would be.}

\ai{Both channel strengths are swept from $0$ to $0.12$ in steps of $0.02$.
Zero checks that noisy and noiseless optimization agree when no error is
present.  The lower part of the range represents weak noise, while the upper
part is a deliberate stress regime where a difference between the two channels
becomes easy to see.  Using the same numerical range makes the comparison
controlled, but equal numbers do not mean that the two errors are equally likely
on hardware: $p_{\mathrm{cx}}$ is the two-qubit depolarizing strength after each
CNOT, whereas $\gamma$ is the amplitude-damping probability applied to each
qubit of that CNOT pair.}

\ai{The two channels are chosen because they test opposite behaviours.  A
\emph{unital} channel leaves the fully mixed state unchanged and does not favour
one state; depolarizing noise is the control case.  A \emph{non-unital} channel
does favour a state; amplitude damping drives qubits toward $|0\rangle$ and can
therefore shift the best VQE angles.  The calculation uses the COBYLA optimizer,
starts from the noiseless angles, and also tries several random starting points.
Table~\ref{tab:noisy} shows the middle-to-strong part of the seed-7 sweep, where
the contrast is clearest.}

\ai{The depolarizing result is important even though almost nothing changes:
at strength $0.08$, re-optimization changes the edge string only from $0.502$ to
$0.501$, while the fidelity remains essentially one.  For this $L=4$ circuit,
the optimizer cannot exploit symmetric depolarizing noise.  This supports the
simpler practice of optimizing ideal angles first when studying this channel.}

\ai{Amplitude damping gives the more cautionary result.  At strength $0.08$,
re-optimization raises the measured edge string from $0.422$ to $0.565$, but at
$0.12$ the fidelity falls from $0.998$ to $0.966$.  The larger displayed signal
therefore does not mean that the ground state was prepared better.  This is the
main achievement of the test: it demonstrates why a noisy VQE result must
include an independent state-quality check.  Reporting only the improved
observable can give the wrong conclusion.}

\ai{Finally, \texttt{NoiseModel.from\_backend} replaces the uniform test channel
with a recorded \texttt{FakeManilaV2} calibration.  Its CNOT error rates vary
from about $0.009$ to $0.014$.  At $\mu=0$ the ideal, backend-calibrated, and
uniform $p_{\mathrm{cx}}=0.05$ edge strings are $0.99$, $0.87$, and $0.67$.
The uniform model is therefore a deliberately harsh comparison, not a device
prediction.  The backend result is still optimistic: its readout errors are not
used because the simulation evaluates
$\operatorname{Tr}(O_{\mathrm{edge}}\rho)$ without measurement instructions.
The data also come from a recorded calibration, not a live hardware run.}

\ai{Together these tests show that realistic noise primarily reduces the
contrast of the non-local diagnostic.  Circuit depth should be chosen by
noiseless expressibility validation followed by noisy evaluation, and any
noise-aware optimization must report a state-quality check rather than only the
observable it was able to improve.  These conclusions concern the $L=4$ NISQ
circuit; their large-$L$ consequences are treated separately in Edgar
Harutyunyan's report.}
