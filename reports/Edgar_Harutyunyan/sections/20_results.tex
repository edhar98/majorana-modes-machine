\section{Methods and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Charge noise versus quasiparticle poisoning}

We first test parity-even charge noise, modelled as local chemical-potential disorder
$\mu_j=\mu_0+U[-W,W]$. Since this channel preserves parity and the quadratic form of the Hamiltonian, a real
antisymmetric $2L\times2L$ Majorana matrix gives the ground-state energy,
covariance matrix, parity gap, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:edge} in $O(L^3)$ time~\cite{leumer2020}. The
calculation used $t=\Delta=1$, $\mu_0=t$, $L=16,32$, 21 disorder strengths, and
300 realisations per point. Here the clean bulk gap is
$\Delta_{\rm bulk}=2t-|\mu_0|=t$. Figure~\ref{fig:disorder-robustness} shows
that at $L=16$ the disorder-averaged edge string changes only from $0.750$ at
$W=0$ to $0.741\pm0.115$ at $W=0.75$, before eroding more strongly near and
above the gap.

  \caption{Disorder-averaged response to charge noise: edge string with one-standard-deviation bands (left) and parity gap (right).}
  \label{fig:disorder-robustness}
\end{figure}

Next, we test parity-odd quasiparticle poisoning using the static boundary term $gX_0$, representing the
left Majorana $c_0+c_0^\dagger$. This Hamiltonian is no longer parity block
diagonal, so a sparse exact diagonalisation of the full $2^{10}$-dimensional
qubit Hamiltonian was used. For a direct comparison, the Hamiltonian with the parity-even charge-noise $Z$ field
was solved in the even sector and averaged over 120 realisations. Figure
\ref{fig:taxonomy} shows that charge noise keeps $\expv{\Pop}=1$, whereas
fermion parity departs continuously from one for any nonzero $g$.
At a matched perturbation strength of $0.30$, the edge strings under charge noise and poisoning are $0.750$
and $0.614$, respectively; at $0.78$, the poisoning result has fallen to $0.318$ with
$\expv{\Pop}=0.54$.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Large-$L$ simulation}

Before choosing a method, we separate three bottlenecks. Dense exact
diagonalisation (ED) stores a $2^L\times2^L=4^L$ Hamiltonian, an ideal circuit
simulation normally stores $2^L$ amplitudes in a statevector, and exact noisy
simulation stores a $4^L$ density matrix. We address these bottlenecks as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \emph{Dense ED $\boldsymbol{\to}$ free fermions.} A free-fermion
  Hamiltonian is quadratic in fermionic operators and diagonalises into
  independent Bogoliubov quasiparticles. A $2L\times2L$ covariance calculation
  then gives the exact reference in $O(L^3)$ time. For $L=4,6,8$, it agrees with
  dense qubit diagonalisation to about $10^{-15}$.
  \item \emph{Statevector $\boldsymbol{\to}$ MPS.} Through $L=16$, sparse
  Pauli operators are evaluated on a statevector. At larger $L$, the same
  circuit state is stored as an MPS.
  \item \emph{Density matrix $\boldsymbol{\to}$ MPS trajectories.} A Kraus
  trajectory is one pure-state realisation of the noisy circuit. Averaging many
  trajectories estimates the noisy expectation without storing the $4^L$
  density matrix~\cite{schollwoeck2011,daley2014}.
\end{enumerate}

At the gapped working point $\mu=0.5t$, fixed-depth scans through $L=16$
found a sharp expressibility threshold: $r=2,3$ failed beyond small chains, but
$r^\ast=4$ kept the noiseless edge string near the exact value
$\expv{\Oedge}\simeq0.94$. Thus the depth needed for this observable did not
grow over the tested range. With a deliberately harsh depolarising rate
$p_{\rm CX}=0.05$, the edge signal fell from $0.681$ at $L=4$ to $0.074\pm0.006$ at $L=16$; 
the recorded \href{https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/en/api/qiskit-ibm-runtime/fake-provider-fake-cairo-v2}{Cairo}
calibration ($p_{2q}=7.53\times10^{-3}$) instead retained $0.523\pm0.005$ at $L=16$.

The final sweep grew the chain in two-site steps, re-optimising the $r=4$
parameters at each step, and used MPS at large $L$. These were simulations rather than hardware runs: recorded
one-qubit noise and a median two-qubit noise channel were applied to the linear
nearest-neighbour circuit without routing it onto the device’s
\href{https://journals.aps.org/prx/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevX.10.011022}{heavy-hex connectivity}~\cite{chamberland2020}.
Each noisy point was obtained from 16,000 trajectories; the error bars show
standard errors calculated from their variance. 
Computations for this work were done (in part) using resources of the Leipzig University Computing Center.

The ideal signal is $0.947$ at $L=6$ and $0.899$ at $L=100$. At $L=64$,
\href{https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/en/api/qiskit-ibm-runtime/fake-provider-fake-brooklyn-v2}{Brooklyn}
(error $1.02\times10^{-2}$) gives $0.301\pm0.035$, whereas
\href{https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/en/api/qiskit-ibm-runtime/fake-provider-fake-washington-v2}{Washington}
(error $1.15\times10^{-2}$) gives $0.198\pm0.035$ and reaches $0.089\pm0.038$ at
$L=100$. 

Recall that a projective measurement of the Pauli string yields $+1$ or $-1$,
with $\expv{\Oedge}=P(+1)-P(-1)$. Thus, the value $0.089$ at $L=100$
corresponds to $54.45\%$ positive and $45.55\%$ negative outcomes, whereas the
ideal contrast is $0.899$. Although the Hamiltonian remains in the topological
regime, decoherence accumulated over the growing gate count leaves the edge
signal barely readable and constitutes the dominant large-$L$ simulation wall.
