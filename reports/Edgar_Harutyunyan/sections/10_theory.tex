\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Protection criterion and diagnostics}

Consider an open chain of $L$ sites labelled $j=0,\ldots,L-1$.
By open boundary conditions (OBC), we mean that there is no bond between sites $L-1$ and $0$.
Under the Jordan--Wigner transformation, the chain is represented by the qubit
Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
 H=-\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}Z_j
 +\frac{t-\Delta}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{L-2}X_jX_{j+1}
 +\frac{t+\Delta}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{L-2}Y_jY_{j+1},
 \qquad \Pop=\prod_{j=0}^{L-1}Z_j .
 \label{eq:qubit-h}
\end{equation}
Here,
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mu$ is the chemical potential;
  \item $t$ is the nearest-neighbour hopping amplitude;
  \item $\Delta$ is the real $p$-wave pairing amplitude;
  \item $X_j$, $Y_j$, and $Z_j$ are Pauli operators acting on qubit $j$.
\end{itemize}


To connect Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubit-h} back to the fermionic variables,
we use $c_j^\dagger$ and $c_j$ for the creation and annihilation operators, respectively, and define
$n_j=c_j^\dagger c_j$ as the occupation-number operator at site $j$.
The code uses the convention
$c_j^\dagger=(\prod_{k<j}Z_k)\sigma_j^+$ with
$2\sigma_j^+=X_j+iY_j$, where $I$ is the single-qubit identity. Hence
$2n_j=I+Z_j$. Defining the total fermion number as $N=\sum_j n_j$, the
fermion-parity operator is
$P_{\mathrm f}=(-1)^N=(-1)^L\prod_j Z_j$.
Thus, the parity convention can be written as
$$P_{\mathrm f}=(-1)^L\Pop=
\begin{cases}
\Pop, & L\ \text{even},\\
-\Pop, & L\ \text{odd}.
\end{cases}$$

For nonzero pairing, the bulk is topological for $|\mu|<2|t|$. The bulk excitation gap
$\Delta_{\mathrm{bulk}}$, defined as the minimum positive quasiparticle energy,
closes at $|\mu|=2|t|$~\cite{kitaev2001}.

Let $V$ denote a perturbation added to the clean Hamiltonian, so
$H'=H+V$. We consider two perturbation families:
$V_{\mathrm{dis}}=\sum_j\delta\mu_j n_j$ with
$\delta\mu_j\in[-W,W]$, and $V_{\mathrm{poison}}=gX_0$.
Thus, $W$ and $g$ set the respective disorder and poisoning strengths.
Appendix~\ref{app:perturbation} derives how these perturbations enter the
Hamiltonian and change its eigenstates and measured observables.
The perturbation $V$ is \emph{local} when it acts
on one site or a finite set of neighbouring sites. It is parity even when
$[V,\Pop]=0$ and parity odd when $\{V,\Pop\}=0$. We therefore require $V$ to be \emph{local}, \emph{parity even}, and \emph{weaker than
$\Delta_{\mathrm{bulk}}$}. Such a perturbation may deform the wavefunction but
cannot mix the logical sectors ($P_{\mathrm f}=\pm1$). Once its strength
approaches the bulk gap, it can create locally trivial regions or close the
bulk gap, thereby destroying the phase. A parity-odd perturbation instead
changes the logical sector directly.
The physical addition or removal of one fermion is called
\emph{quasiparticle poisoning}; increasing $L$ does not suppress this logical
error~\cite{rainis2012}.

To define the measured diagnostic, we introduce the Majorana operators
$\gamma_{2j}=c_j+c_j^\dagger=(\prod_{k<j}Z_k)X_j$ and
$\gamma_{2j+1}=i(c_j-c_j^\dagger)=(\prod_{k<j}Z_k)Y_j$. The logical quantum
number is the fermion parity $P_{\mathrm f}$ ($\Pop$ for even $L$), while the
finite-chain edge diagnostic used in the simulations is
\begin{equation}
 \Oedge=X_0Z_1\cdots Z_{L-2}X_{L-1}
       =-i\gamma_1\gamma_{2L-2}.
 \label{eq:edge}
\end{equation}
This edge-string operator is a bilinear of the two boundary Majorana operators.
Its expectation value is large for the prepared finite-chain state in the
topological regime, whereas it is suppressed in the trivial regime. This makes
it a useful finite-size diagnostic rather than an independent bulk topological
invariant. A poisoning event always changes fermion parity, whereas
its immediate effect on this particular string depends on the event's site and
Pauli component. In summary, parity is the universal indicator of poisoning,
whereas $\Oedge$ measures whether the prepared edge correlation survives.

The taxonomy transfers under Jordan--Wigner, but the transmon does not inherit
the wire's gap protection. This emulator limitation is quantified by comparing
the noisy and ideal versions of the same circuit:
$\epsilon_{\rm edge}=|\expv{\Oedge}_{\rm noisy}-\expv{\Oedge}_{\rm ideal}|$.
In Qiskit, two-qubit gate noise mixes parity-even charge/bond errors and
parity-odd poisoning errors~\cite{qiskit2024}.

\subsection{Scaling to large \texorpdfstring{$L$}{L}}

Large-$L$ behaviour is governed by competing intrinsic and circuit scales.
On the $t=\Delta$ line, the clean Majorana splitting in the topological regime
obeys~\cite{kitaev2001,leumer2020}
\begin{equation}
 \delta E(L)\simeq2|t|(1-\lambda^2)\lambda^L,
 \qquad \lambda=\frac{|\mu|}{2|t|}=e^{-1/\xi},
 \label{eq:intrinsic-scaling}
\end{equation}
so increasing $L$ exponentially suppresses edge-mode hybridisation. For a
depth-$r$ linear entangler, however, $n_{\rm CX}=r(L-1)$. A simple envelope for
independent two-qubit errors therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 |\expv{\Oedge}_{\rm noisy}|\sim|\expv{\Oedge}_{\rm ideal}|
 (1-p_{\rm CX})^{r(L-1)}\simeq|\expv{\Oedge}_{\rm ideal}|
 e^{-p_{\rm CX}r(L-1)}.
 \label{eq:circuit-scaling}
\end{equation}
The gate-level origin of this envelope and the $L=4$ depth optimum are analysed
in \href{https://edhar98.github.io/majorana-modes-machine/reports/Dobromir_Stoev/report.pdf}{D.~Stoev's report};
here, we use the envelope only to study the dependence on $L$ after fixing the
shallowest adequately expressive depth.
At fixed $r$, a nearest-neighbour circuit has bond dimension
$\chi\leq2^r$ and MPS memory $O(L\chi^2)$~\cite{schollwoeck2011}; see
Appendix~\ref{app:mps-memory}. Thus, increasing $L$ improves the intrinsic
protection of the logical wire but simultaneously makes its circuit diagnostic
more vulnerable, provided that the fixed depth remains expressive.
